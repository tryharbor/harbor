**For a small brand, the metrics that matter are the ones that show people chose to do something: saves, shares, replies, profile visits and link clicks, read against your own median at the same age.** Views tell you how far a post traveled. Followers and likes tell you very little on their own.

Here's how to read each one, where to find them, and a simple weekly check.

## Three questions, not thirty numbers

Every metric answers one of three questions:

1. **Did people see it?** Views, and on Instagram, reach.
2. **Did they care?** Saves, shares, replies, reposts.
3. **Did they come closer?** Profile visits, link clicks, follows from the post.

A post can do well on one and badly on another. A post with modest views and lots of saves is often more valuable than a post with big views and nothing else.

## The metrics, ranked for a small brand

### Saves and bookmarks

Someone wanted to come back to it. That's the clearest sign a post was useful. Instagram calls them saves; on X, bookmarks.

### Shares and reposts

Someone was willing to put their name next to it. Shares are how a small account reaches people who don't follow it yet.

### Replies

Read them, don't just count them. Questions tell you what to post next. Objections tell you what to explain better. A post with five real questions beats one with fifty emoji replies.

### Profile visits and link clicks

Someone wanted more than the post. If you sell something, these are closest to revenue. A post that sends profile visits but no follows usually means your profile doesn't say clearly what you do.

### Views and reach

Views count how many times the post was shown; reach (on Instagram) counts unique accounts. Use them to judge distribution, not quality. They're also the number to compare across posts at the same age, because almost everything else scales with them.

### Likes

The cheapest action. Useful as a tiebreaker, not a goal.

### Followers

A slow, lagging total. Watch the trend monthly. Day to day, it mostly reflects one or two posts that traveled.

## Where to find them

Each app shows most of these in its own post analytics, under slightly different names: X calls saves "bookmarks", LinkedIn calls views "impressions", Instagram splits views from reach.

If you post through the platforms' APIs, the picture differs again. X, Instagram and Threads return per-post numbers, while LinkedIn's post analytics need a separately approved API product. I wrote up [what every social API gives you](/blog/what-every-social-api-gives-you-in-2026) in more detail.

## Read every number the same way

Two habits make these numbers trustworthy:

**Compare at the same age.** A post that's been up a week will beat a post that's been up six hours on every metric. Write down each post's numbers at a fixed point, such as 24 hours, and compare those. On X, where most of a post's life is early, also look at 1 hour.

**Compare to your own median.** Divide each post's 24-hour number by the median of your recent posts on that platform. 1.5 means 50% better than your usual. This works for an account with 300 followers and one with 300,000, and it stops one viral outlier from distorting the picture.

If you run more than one brand, keep a separate median for each. A number that's a great day for one account can be a slow day for another.

## A weekly check that takes 15 minutes

Once a week, for each platform:

1. List the week's posts with views, saves, shares and replies at 24 hours.
2. Mark every post above 1.0x your median views.
3. For those, ask what they had in common: format, topic, opening line, time.
4. Read the replies on your two best posts and write down every question.
5. Plan next week: one more of what worked, one post that answers a question people asked.

That's the whole system. Most of the value is in step 4.

## What to ignore

- **Engagement rate as a single number.** Formulas differ by tool and by platform, so the same post can show three different rates. Look at the actions themselves.
- **Industry benchmarks.** An average across thousands of accounts says little about yours. Your own median is the benchmark that matters.
- **Day-to-day follower changes.** Too noisy to act on.
- **Vanity totals in screenshots.** A total without the post's age attached can't be compared.

A disclosure: I build Harbor, an open-source tool that measures each post at 1 hour, 6 hours, 24 hours, 3 days and 7 days against the brand's own median. The method above doesn't need it; a spreadsheet works.

## FAQ

### What is the most important social media metric for a small business?

Saves and shares, read against your own median. They show a post was useful enough to keep or pass on, which matters more than raw views for an account that's still growing.

### Is engagement rate a good metric?

As a rough signal only. Tools calculate it differently (by followers, by views or by reach), so compare the actions themselves, like saves, shares and replies, rather than one combined rate.

### How do I compare posts fairly?

Compare them at the same age, for example 24 hours after posting, and divide each one by your median for that platform. That removes both the age difference and the size of your account from the comparison.

### How often should I check my metrics?

Once a week is enough for most small brands. Daily checks mostly show noise; a weekly look shows which posts beat your median and what people asked.

### Do followers matter?

Over months, yes, as a sign the account is growing. Week to week, they lag behind what your posts are doing, so judge posts by what people did with them instead.
