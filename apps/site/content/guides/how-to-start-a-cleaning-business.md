Starting a cleaning business is one of the cheapest ways to work for yourself: no premises, little equipment, and customers in every neighborhood. Here are the steps in order, with what each one costs.


The same steps apply to any local service business; [how to start a service business](/guides/how-to-start-a-service-business) covers them for every trade.

## 1. Decide what you'll clean, and where

Start with **residential cleaning**: homes are easier to win than offices, and clients book again and again. Offer three things clearly:

- **Standard clean:** the regular visit.
- **Deep clean:** a first visit or a seasonal one, which takes much longer.
- **Move-out clean:** for tenants and landlords between leases.

Pick a service area you can drive around easily. Clients close together mean more cleans a day and less time on the road.

## 2. Set your prices

In late 2026 a standard clean typically goes for about **$146–$202**, a deep clean **$190–$265** and a move-out clean **$200–$286** (Thumbtack, October 2026). Work out what you need from each job (your hourly pay, driving, supplies and card fees) and price within those ranges.

[How much to charge for house cleaning →](/guides/how-much-to-charge-for-house-cleaning)

## 3. Register the business

- **Sole proprietor:** if you just start trading under your own name, you're a sole proprietor automatically. There's nothing to file and it's free (SBA).
- **EIN:** a free tax number from the IRS. It's handy for a business bank account even if you don't have employees.
- **LLC (optional):** it keeps business debts separate from you. Filing costs $125 in Florida, $200 in New York and $300 in Texas, and some states charge a yearly fee too.
- **Business license:** most states don't have a special license for cleaning, but your city or county may require a general business license. California has a janitorial registration with the Labor Commissioner, so check whether it applies to you.

## 4. Get insured

Liability insurance covers you if you damage something in a client's home. For a small cleaning business it costs about **$42–$51 a month** (Simply Business; Insureon, 2026). A **janitorial bond**, which covers clients against theft, costs about **$112–$175 a year**. Many clients ask about both, and "insured and bonded" is worth putting on your website and ads.

## 5. Buy your supplies

A full one-person kit costs about **$312**. That covers microfiber cloths, a mop system, spray bottles, a caddy, all-purpose cleaner, glass cleaner, disinfectant, bathroom cleaner, gloves and trash bags. Start with your own vacuum, or budget about **$446** for a commercial upright.

[The full itemized budget →](/guides/cleaning-business-startup-costs)

## 6. Set up how you'll get paid

Decide on **cash, bank transfer or card**. Card payments cost about 2.6%–3.3% plus a few cents each (Stripe; Square), and they make it easy for clients to pay on the day or by invoice. Keep business money in its own account from the first job: it makes taxes much simpler.

## 7. Plan for taxes

As a self-employed person you pay **self-employment tax of 15.3%** on your net earnings, plus income tax. The IRS expects estimated payments during the year: for 2026 they're due April 15, June 15, September 15 and January 15, 2027. Setting aside a share of every payment means no surprises.

## 8. Get your first clients

Tell everyone you know, set up a free **Google Business Profile** (you can hide your home address and list the areas you serve), and ask every client for a review and a referral.

[A four-week plan for your first 10 clients →](/guides/get-your-first-cleaning-clients)

## 9. Get a simple website

You don't need one for your first few clients, but you will once people start searching for your business name. Keep it simple: what you clean, your areas, your prices or "from" prices, "insured and bonded", and a way to get in touch. Harbor can write one from a description of your business, with a contact form that sends every enquiry to you.

## Check whether you charge sales tax

Some states tax cleaning services, including homes. Check yours before you set prices, because the tax goes on top of your price:

| State | Residential cleaning |
|---|---|
| Texas | Taxable. A narrow exemption for someone working as a household's own employee |
| New York | Taxable, unless the cleaner is the household's employee |
| Ohio | Taxable (homes, offices and other buildings) once your sales of the service reach $5,000 a year |
| Pennsylvania | Taxable, residential and commercial |
| Iowa | Not taxable (Iowa taxes non-residential cleaning only) |

If your state taxes cleaning, register for a sales tax permit with your state revenue department before your first job.

## Hiring help

When you're ready for a second pair of hands, decide first whether they're an **employee or a contractor**. The IRS looks at three things: who controls how the work is done, who controls the money side (tools, expenses, how they're paid), and the type of relationship. Someone you train, schedule and equip is usually an employee.

- **Contractors:** for payments made in 2026, you file a Form 1099-NEC once you've paid someone **$2,000** or more in the year. The threshold was $600 before 2026 (IRS Publication 15).
- **Employees:** workers' compensation insurance is mandatory for most employers in every state except Texas (NAIC). Payroll taxes and withholding apply too.

## Keep business money and records separate

The IRS advises keeping your business bank account separate from your personal one (Publication 583), and the SBA says the same. It makes your income, costs and taxes far easier to see. Keep receipts and records for **at least three years**, and four years for anything to do with employment taxes (IRS).

## FAQ

### How much does it cost to start a cleaning business?

About $370 for a lean start (a supplies kit, your own vacuum and a month of insurance), about $1,200–$1,400 for a typical setup with a commercial vacuum, a bond and an LLC, and about $2,200 for a fuller launch with a year of insurance and some ads.

### Do I need a license to start a cleaning business?

Usually not a cleaning-specific one, but many cities and counties require a general business license. Check your city or county website, and in California check the janitorial registration rules.

### Is a cleaning business profitable?

It can be, because the costs are low: mostly your time, driving and supplies. The going rate for a standard clean is about $146–$202 (Thumbtack, October 2026), so price each job to cover your pay, costs and taxes.

### Should I start with homes or offices?

Homes are a good place to start: you can win them through referrals and local search, and weekly or every-other-week clients make the income steady. Offices often want contracts, evening hours and more staff.
