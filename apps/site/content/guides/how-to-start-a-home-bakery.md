Each of the 13 states below lets you sell some baked goods made in your home kitchen, under what are called **cottage food laws**. The rules differ a lot from state to state: what you can bake, where you can sell, how much, and what goes on the label. Start there, then the rest is like any small business.

The same steps apply to any local service business; [how to start a service business](/guides/how-to-start-a-service-business) covers them for every trade.

## 1. Check your state's cottage food law

This decides almost everything else. The rules in 13 states, from each state's agriculture or health department:

| State | Permit and fee | Yearly sales cap | Online orders and shipping |
|---|---|---|---|
| California | County registration (Class A) or permit (Class B); fees set by county | $88,878 (A) or $177,756 (B) in 2026 | Online orders OK; mail or delivery within California |
| Colorado | None until 2027; registration required from January 1, 2027 | $10,000 per product; $150,000 in total from 2027 | Online orders OK, within Colorado |
| Florida | None | $250,000 | Online and mail order allowed |
| Georgia | None since July 2025 | Not stated | Direct, at events and online |
| Illinois | Local registration, up to $50 a year | None | Online and shipping within Illinois |
| Michigan | None | $50,000 ($75,000 if items sell for $250 or more) | Online and mail order within Michigan |
| New Jersey | $100 permit for 2 years | $50,000 | Online orders OK; no shipping, deliver in person |
| New York | Free registration | Not stated | Online, within New York only |
| North Carolina | Home kitchen inspection required first | Not stated | Not stated |
| Ohio | None (a $10 Home Bakery license for refrigerated items) | Not stated | Within Ohio only |
| Pennsylvania | $35 registration after an inspection, $35 a year | Not stated | Online sales from your home allowed |
| Texas | None; register with the state only to sell refrigerated foods | $150,000 | Online orders OK; deliver in person, no shipping |
| Washington | $355 permit for 2 years, with an inspection | $35,000 | Online orders OK; no shipping, deliver in person |

If your state isn't listed, search for "[your state] cottage food" and look for the agriculture or health department's page.

## 2. Choose what you'll bake

Many states allow only foods that **don't need refrigeration**: breads, cookies, cakes with shelf-stable frosting, brownies, muffins and fruit pies. Cheesecake, cream and custard fillings, and cream cheese frosting are excluded in New York, Pennsylvania, North Carolina, Washington and Michigan, and in Ohio unless you get a Home Bakery license. Colorado doesn't currently allow buttercream made with butter, though ghee or vegetable-oil buttercream is fine. **Texas** allows refrigerated foods if you register and keep them cold, and **Illinois** allows them if they're kept at 41°F or below.

Start with a short menu of things you make well, in a few sizes.

## 3. Set up your kitchen

Some states have rules about the home itself:

- **California:** no infants, small children or pets in the kitchen while you're baking or packaging for sale.
- **North Carolina:** if a pet comes into the home at any time, even only at night, you can't make food for sale there.
- **Washington:** a pet management plan if you have pets, and an inspection before your permit.
- **Ohio's Home Bakery license:** no pets in the home.

## 4. Take a food safety course

Many states require training. Texas and Washington require a food handler card; a ServSafe online food handler course is **$15**. Illinois and New Jersey require a **food protection manager** certificate instead, which typically costs $100–$300 (University of Illinois Extension). California requires a cottage food operator course within three months of registering.

## 5. Label every item

Labels usually need the product name, ingredients, net weight, your name and address (or permit number), the **allergens**, and a statement that it was made in a home kitchen. Some states' exact wording:

- **California:** "Made in a Home Kitchen," in 12-point type.
- **Florida:** "Made in a cottage food operation that is not subject to Florida's food safety regulations."
- **Texas:** "This product was produced in a private residence that is not subject to governmental licensing or inspection."
- **Michigan:** "Made in a home kitchen that has not been inspected by the Michigan Department of Agriculture & Rural Development."

The FDA lists nine major food allergens: **milk, eggs, fish, crustacean shellfish, tree nuts, peanuts, wheat, soybeans and sesame**. Sesame was added in 2023, so older templates may miss it.

## 6. Get insured

Product liability insurance covers you if someone gets sick or has an allergic reaction. The Food Liability Insurance Program's baked goods policy starts at **$299 a year** (about $26 a month) for $1 million per claim. Insureon's figure for cake and cookie bakers is about $39 a month for general liability.

## 7. Set your prices

Price from your ingredients, packaging and time. Couples paid an average of **$540** for a wedding cake (The Knot, 2026), and a wedding cake for 30 or fewer guests averages **$230** on Thumbtack.

## 8. Register the business

You're a **sole proprietor** by default, at no cost, and an EIN from the IRS is free. An LLC is optional: $125 to file in Florida, $200 in New York and $300 in Texas. Your city may also require a general business license; check its website.

## 9. Win your first customers

Start with people you know, take pre-orders for holidays, and try a local farmers market. In states that allow it (including New York, North Carolina, Georgia and Ohio), local cafes and shops can stock your goods.

## 10. Get a simple website

A website gives people one place to see your menu, prices and pickup or delivery days, and to order. Harbor can write one from a description of your bakery, with an order form that sends every request to you.

## Check whether you charge sales tax

Bakery items are often exempt as food, but not always:

| State | Baked goods |
|---|---|
| Texas | Not taxable from a bakery (over half its sales are bakery items, sold to take away); taxable with eating utensils from other sellers |
| California | Food products are generally not taxable |
| New York | Bread, cookies and cupcakes are exempt from food stores; food to eat on the premises isn't |
| Pennsylvania | Cakes, pies and bread are exempt from a bakery or food retailer; cookies sold at fairs are taxable |

Check your state's revenue department, especially if you sell at events.

## FAQ

### Do I need a license to sell baked goods from home?

It depends on your state. Florida, Texas, Georgia and Michigan need no permit for most baked goods. Others require registration (New York, free) or a permit (Pennsylvania $35, New Jersey $100, Washington $355). Check your state's cottage food page.

### Can I sell cakes with cream cheese frosting from home?

In many states, no: it needs refrigeration. Texas allows it if you register with the state and keep it cold, and Illinois allows it if it's kept at 41°F or below. New York, Pennsylvania, North Carolina, Washington and Michigan exclude it.

### Can I ship baked goods from home?

In some states. Florida allows mail order, Michigan within the state, and Illinois within the state for shelf-stable items. Texas, Washington and New Jersey don't allow shipping; you deliver in person.

### How much can a home bakery make?

Many states cap yearly sales: $35,000 in Washington, $50,000 in New Jersey and Michigan, $150,000 in Texas and $250,000 in Florida. Illinois has no cap. For context, bakers' median pay was $37,160 a year in May 2025 (BLS).
